Of Curves and Hands

What does it mean to translate a ritual without flattening it?

Of Curves and Hands begins with kolam-making, a practice held in the body as much as in the drawn line. The work asks how touch, rhythm, repetition, and care can move through sensors, code, and mechanical drawing while still carrying cultural memory.

KOLAM The story begins at the threshold. Across South India and other parts of Asia, kolams are drawn daily at the entrances of homes and temples: geometric line drawings made by hand using rice flour or rice paste.

They are both ritual and offering, welcoming prosperity and positive energy while sustaining small creatures such as ants and birds.

Their complexity is not only visual. A kolam carries bodily knowledge: practiced hand movements, rhythm, repetition, pressure, and material sensitivity passed down through generations.


ANATOMY OF A KOLAM This work grows out of Anatomy of a Kolam, an ongoing participatory design research project exploring how kolam-making can be reinterpreted through contemporary computational and mechanical systems without losing its embodied, ritual, and cultural depth.

The research sits between speculative design, physical computing, and interactive experience. Rather than asking whether a machine can reproduce a kolam, it asks how human intention, gesture, and machine logic might co-exist in the act of making one.



PROTOTYPE ISLAND In Milan, the project entered Prototype Island, an exhibition presented by the DesignSingapore Council that frames Singapore as a living prototype: a nation that is never static, but continuously evolving, testing, and adapting through design.

Led by curator Hunn Wai with assistant curator Eian Siew and global perspectives advisor Maria Cristina Didero, Prototype Island brought together 15 transdisciplinary projects by Singapore-based designers across technology, materiality, care, craft, and urban living futures. Within this context, Of Curves and Hands became a question of cultural continuity: how might a practice rooted in everyday ritual continue to evolve through new tools?

The exhibition debuted at Milan Design Week from April 21-26, 2026 at Foro Buonaparte 54 in the Brera Design District, and received the inaugural Fuorisalone Award Special Mention for Essere Progetto / Being the Project. Its Singapore homecoming ran from July 24-September 20, 2026 at ArtScience Museum, B2 Circulation.

A KOLAM IN TRANSLATION Of Curves and Hands: A Kolam in Translation is the site-specific iteration that emerged from this broader inquiry.

Visitors begin with their hands. Through embodied, hand-led interaction, their movements are captured as input, translated through a computational system, and rendered physically by a mechanical drawing setup. Gesture becomes both the interface and the meaning of the work.




GESTURAL TRACES What remains is not a perfect reproduction of tradition, but a trace of encounter. The plotted drawings preserve unevenness, rhythm, improvisation, and evidence of touch. Each interaction produces a tangible outcome while also contributing to a growing collective archive shaped by many hands.

The project returns to its opening question through making. What is gained, and what risks being lost, when rituals rooted in touch, care, and repetition are mediated by technology? Of Curves and Hands proposes interaction not as spectacle alone, but as a form of shared, embodied making.
